## situations

### authority
Your phone rings, and the screen shows your bank's name. The caller says they are from the bank's fraud team. They know your name and your address, and they say someone is trying to take money from your account. To stop it, they ask you to read out the code your bank has just sent you by text.
Read them the code. They knew your details, so they must be the bank.
Ask them to prove they are from the bank before you give the code.
Hang up, and call the number on the back of your card.
A scammer can make any name or number show on your phone when they call, including your bank's, and may already know things about you, such as your name. If you ask for proof, they can only give you more details like these, which a scammer can have too. A code your bank sends you is for you to type in yourself, and anyone who asks you to tell them the code is a scammer. The number on the back of your card is your bank's own number, so calling it is how you reach the real fraud team.

### urgency
A caller says they are from your internet provider. They say a virus on your computer is attacking their network, and that your connection will be cut off in 30 minutes unless you install a program that lets them fix it.
Install the program, so the connection is not cut off.
Stay on the call and ask them to explain the problem first.
Hang up, and call your provider on the number on its website or your bill.
The 30-minute deadline is there to make you act before you have time to check. A program a caller asks you to install can let them control your computer and see what you type. If there really is a problem, your provider can tell you when you call it on the number from its website or your bill. Staying on the line only gives the caller more time to persuade you.

### trust
You get a message from a friend's account on a messaging app, saying they are away from home, their wallet has been stolen and they need you to send money today. The message is written just the way your friend usually writes.
Send the money. The message comes from their account and sounds like them.
Reply in the same chat and ask a question only they would know.
Call them on the number you already have for them.
Someone else can take over your friend's account and use it to ask your friend's contacts for money. On apps that keep your chats online, they can also read your past messages, so they can write the way your friend does and answer a question only your friend should know. When you call the number you already had for your friend, you speak to your friend and not to whoever has the account.

### fear
An email says the sender recorded you through your webcam and will send the video to everyone you know unless you pay within 24 hours. As proof, the email shows a password you once used.
Pay, so the video is not sent.
Reply and ask them to show you the video first.
Do not reply or pay, and change that password anywhere you still use it.
Emails like this are usually sent to huge numbers of people at once, and the password in them usually comes from a data breach, not from your computer. Replying tells the sender that someone reads that email address, and paying tells them you are someone who pays. If someone really does have an image of you and threatens to share it, the second step below says what you can do and who to tell.

### scarcity
A website you have never heard of sells the phone you want for half the usual price. A banner on the website says only 2 are left and that the price ends in 10 minutes.
Buy it now, before the price ends.
Buy it, but pay by card rather than by bank transfer.
Leave it, and read reviews of the website on other websites before you decide.
A countdown and a low stock count are there to make you pay before you have checked the website. Fake shopping websites copy the look of real ones and take your money without sending anything. Paying by card can make it easier to get your money back than a bank transfer does, but it does not make the website real.

### reciprocity
Someone you play an online game with has been friendly for weeks and given you free items. Now they send you a link to a website where you can collect a bigger gift, if you sign in with your game account.
Sign in on that website. They have been generous, so it seems fair.
Sign in, then change your password straight after.
Turn the gift down, and only ever sign in through the game itself.
When someone gives you something first, you feel you owe them something back, which makes it harder to say no to what they ask next. A sign-in page on a link someone sends you can look exactly like the game's real one, and the password you type into it goes straight to whoever made the page. Some scammers sign in within half an hour of getting a password and change it so you cannot get back in, which is why changing your password afterwards can be too late.

### social
Everyone in a group chat you are in is talking about an investment app that they say doubled their money. A friend posts a picture of their earnings.
Join. If they all made money, it must work.
Put in a small amount first to see if it pays out.
Look the app up somewhere other than the chat, and ask your friend about it by phone or in person.
A picture of earnings is easy to fake, and other people in the group who say they made money can be part of the scam. These scams often let you take out a small amount at first, so that you trust them and put in more, and then stop you taking out the rest. Ask your friend outside the chat, because someone else may be using their account.
