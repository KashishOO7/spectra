
## title
Someone may be watching me through my phone or computer

## subtitle
Someone may have installed software to watch you

## do-not
Do not confront the person, and do not remove anything yet. Removing monitoring software tells them you know, and that is when these situations escalate. Talk to a specialist first.


## technical
- Do not confront the person you suspect, and do not remove anything yet. Removal is what tells them they have been found, and that is the moment these situations escalate.
- Treat the device as watched: assume its screen, keystrokes and location are visible. From now on, search and plan on another device, not this one.
- Move to a device the person you suspect has never had access to, such as a friend's phone.
- Preserve what you have before changing anything: photograph unfamiliar apps, notification history, and any messages showing they knew something they should not. Store it in an account they do not know about. Resetting the device now would erase the apps you need to show the specialist.
- Contact a tech safety specialist, for example at a domestic violence service in your country, and describe what you have seen. They can advise you on when and how to remove what you found, and on keeping the evidence.
- When the specialist says it is safe, change your passwords from a device the person you suspect has never had access to, starting with your email, because the reset links for your other accounts go there. Then change your banking password, then the rest.
- On iPhone, Safety Check can then stop sharing with other people and sign other devices out of your Apple Account in one place. Search Settings for Safety Check. The person you suspect may notice that you stopped sharing your location or other information.
- Turn on two-factor authentication as you go, and check account recovery options. A phone number or backup email the other person controls lets them straight back in.

## plain
- Do not say anything to the person you suspect, and do not delete anything yet. Deleting it is what tells them you know, and that is when things often get worse.
- Assume the device is being watched: its screen, your typing and your location. From now on, search and make plans on another device, not this one.
- Use a different device that the person you suspect has never had access to, such as a friend's phone.
- Before you change anything, save proof: take photos of apps you do not recognise, and of any message showing they knew something they should not have. Put it somewhere they cannot reach. Resetting the device now would erase the apps you need to show.
- Before you remove anything or change any settings, contact a tech safety specialist, for example at a domestic violence service in your country. They can tell you when and how to remove what you found.
- When the specialist says it is safe, use a device the person you suspect has never had access to, and change your email password first, because the reset links for your other accounts go there. Then change your banking password, then the rest.
- On iPhone, Safety Check can then stop sharing with other people and sign other devices out of your Apple Account in one place. Search Settings for Safety Check. The person you suspect may notice that you stopped sharing your location or other information.
- Turn on two-factor authentication while you are there, and check the recovery phone number and backup email on each account. If either one is theirs, change it.
